# Ear notes — what the untrained ear could and couldn't do

Field data for TheoryOfMagic's scored-encounters doctrine
(`E:\experiments\TheoryOfMagic\Docs\design\wiki\music-mechanics.md`), and for
the eventual conversation with a real composer. The instrument taking these
measurements — the player's untrained ear — is being retrained by the same
curriculum that produces them, so a finding not written down is lost the day
the lesson lands. Date every entry, name what surfaced it, keep it short.

Doctrine assumptions under test: same-vs-different recognition as "the
strongest untrained faculty"; octave-completion as a felt countdown;
direction → fan chirality.

Method note: the drill format (answer a label, get marked) trains the
translation and leaves the sensation undescribed — it destroys the data this
file exists to collect. For research passes: play it, have the player
describe it in their own words, name the concept only afterwards.

---

**2026-08-11 · direction is a label, not a percept.** L4.1 contour echoes,
then 23 quiz answers: 20/23 right, every miss a direction flip, same-note
detection 4/4 and among the fastest on the board, interval size did *not*
predict difficulty. Median reaction time 365 ms right vs 1029 ms wrong —
**no fast answer was ever wrong.** Player: *"Again cannot possibly be
confused. It feels completely different from everything else."*

Reading: up/down is a learned metaphor (other languages map pitch to
thin/thick, big/small). The pair is heard fine; what is slow and
error-prone is translating it into the spatial label, and that is where the
errors live. Player's own account of the confusing cases (C5→C4, F4→C4):
a felt curve that "rises before falling", i.e. the arrival, not the
direction, is what registers.

Doctrine: register 1 **confirmed and stronger than claimed** — sameness is
categorically separate, not merely accurate. Direction→chirality is doubly
weak (unreliable *and* culturally mediated) — never build a "which way did
it go" read without a redundant channel. Player ruling: up/down stays as
the working frame anyway; the value is knowing it is scaffolding.

**2026-08-11 · what a delay is worth, ranked (the strongest dataset yet).**
Free-play page in C, a descending line arriving G4 → C4. The player delayed
the final C and ranked every filler note across two octaves. Silence in the
gap: *"absolute warcrime… the most clear NO I've experienced in the entirety
of this project"* — it withholds the resolution **and** the pulse, and losing
the clock is the worse half.

Two thresholds, not one:
- **Near:** within ~3 semitones of the target the filler stops being an
  event and becomes decoration. Player's word for a whole-step filler was
  "rhythmic" — below some pitch distance the pitch channel reports nothing
  and only timing is left.
- **Far:** rejected notes were exactly those outside the range the piece had
  already claimed (~A3–G4), not those past some fixed interval. "Too far" is
  **territorial, not metric.** One exception, G3 — just under the floor but
  the strongest relation to the tonic.
- **Trajectory:** everything above the departure note G4 was bad, and
  monotonically less bad the closer it came back down. A filler above the
  departure abandons the descent instead of delaying it.

Both prior theories lost. The assistant predicted the leading tone (B3)
would win on tendency — the player rated it the flattest option on the
board. The player predicted shared harmonics would win — both G's did well
but so did F4 and E4, which are not fifths.

Doctrine: this is the tension→release unit model reached from the ear side
— the same conclusion research-agenda item 1 reached from the analysis
side, now by two independent routes. Also: **the pulse is load-bearing and
the pitch drama rides on it.** Breaking the clock is a harder NO than any
pitch choice, which argues for rhythm/negative-space mechanics (register 4)
over pitch-shape mechanics for untrained players.

**Smaller findings, same session.**
- **ABAB vs ABAC.** Player, unprompted: ABAB carries "enormous repetition
  tension", ABAC none. Repetition is a promise; an asserted pattern demands
  continuation or break. This is register 3 ("repeats but different")
  felt directly, and it is *loud* for this player — the same
  same/different faculty as "again", applied to spans instead of pitches.
- **Streams, not notes.** Writing a pedal (C between each excursion) split
  into two voices for the player automatically, and direction was judged
  against the *previous excursion*, not the ground. Reference for a
  judgement is the last landmark in the same stream.
- **Scale membership is a weak term.** Claimed by the assistant as a
  categorical channel, then tested by the player: swapping a tritone for a
  minor third removed most of the difference between an in-scale and an
  out-of-scale neighbour. Interval quality is immediate and context-free;
  membership needs a real key to bite and a quick chromatic ornament is not
  enough to make it bite. Claim downgraded.

`OPEN`, all cheap: does the octave feel closer to "again" than a fifth
does? Do descents landing *off* the tonic read more clearly than
tonic-landings? Does the far threshold move if the piece's range is widened
first (territorial vs metric)?

**2026-08-28 · the stacked walk beats its skeleton, immediately.** First
day of real chord-lane listening (L5 free play). The player laid a triad
on every other step and walked the degree straight up — deg 0 through 8,
the whole chord-scale in order — then judged it against "the equivalent
of just the roots": *"this to me sounds a lot better."* Unprompted
experiment, clean design: same contour, same rhythm, the only variable is
one line versus three built-from-the-key.

What the ear is getting: roots-only varies in height alone; the walked
triads also vary in internal spacing step to step (the key's uneven
steps baked into each stack), so shape shifts under the climb. The
untrained ear registers that second channel instantly and prefers it —
consistent with the interval-quality-is-immediate finding above
(vertical spacing read as color, no training required). Notably the
player had *proposed* the scale-of-chords idea in conversation minutes
earlier and distrusted it as a sampling artifact; the grid experiment is
what settled it. Relational hearing again: the finding arrived as a
comparison, never as a name.

**Same session, minutes later: crossing is cool.** The player set the
tune walking down a full octave against the climbing chord-scale —
contrary directions, crossing mid-page — and liked it unprompted
(*"crossing them's kinda cool though"*). Both lines are plain stairs;
what the ear enjoys is the changing distance between them: closing,
meeting, reopening. Third finding in one sitting where the pleasure
lives in a *relation between streams* rather than in either stream —
the relational-hearing profile predicting taste, not just perception.

**A/B confirmed, then a third stream.** The player ran the suggested
control: same two walks in parallel motion — *"it has none of that cool
'fuga' feel."* Their own word, and a precise one: the pleasure of the
crossing version is pursuit, one line fleeing while the other closes.
Then they added a simple ostinato bassline (L2 material) *"and it added
something"*: the two walks both go somewhere, the loop refuses to — a
fixed point both moving lines are now measured against. Three streams,
three live pairwise relations, assembled unprompted in one free-play
sitting. The relational-taste finding keeps compounding: every addition
they liked today added a *relation*, not a sound.

**2026-08-29 · a chord cannot be wrong alone.** Player, after a session
on the chord lane: *"the chords tool doesn't really ever sound wrong.
it can be not excellent, but very rarely wrong."* Accurate, and the
reason generalizes: a single note has no frame and can only be wrong
against something; a chord carries its own frame — a three-note excerpt
of the key that justifies itself. Wrongness needs two frames
disagreeing. The lane compounds it with three rails (in-key deg dial,
curated shapes, near-voicing smoothing every join): solo chord-lane
output is always grammatical; the live axis is excellent vs dull.
Mechanic consequence (ToM-relevant): a chords-only instrument is
unlosable and reads as low-stakes; stakes enter exactly when an
independent line can disagree with the frame — the held note across a
chord change is the smallest possible wrongness generator.

**2026-08-29 · the sequence, found by hand.** In wide-field the
player built cells whose roots (1-based) run 5·6·4·5·3·4·2 — a
two-chord move (root up one degree, then down a third) copied one
degree lower each repeat: a sequence, one of the oldest devices there
is, arrived at by taste before the name. Predicted by the relational
profile twice over: the repeated object is not a chord but an interval
pattern *between* chords (+1, −3), root-agnostic by construction; and
the same page's second half runs the device inverted (root walking up
in alternation with a fixed V) apparently unnoticed. The pleasure
mechanism fits the running theory — the copy makes each landing
predicted, and prediction-confirmed-at-a-new-height is a relation, not
a sound.

**2026-08-29 · the neighbor dip.** The part the player actually
flagged as liked ("*this middle part - does it have a name?*") was
elsewhere on the same page: ii struck four times on alternating cells,
one dip to I, back to ii, then the same plateau one degree up on iii.
The named thing inside it is neighbor motion (I as neighbor chord —
leave by a step, return at once); the repetition itself is just static
harmony re-articulated. Notable for the profile: what drew their ear
was again a relation — the dip-and-return that makes a repeated chord
feel re-chosen rather than stuck — and they found the plateau pacing
(harmonic rhythm stretched, then stepwise motion between plateaus)
without being shown it. Refinement, in their words: "*dip before
motion is what i'm finding cool about it*" — not the neighbor itself
but its placement, immediately before the rise, where the down-move
reads as a wind-up for the up-move. The timing has no name in theory;
the sensitivity is to a directional relation (contrary approach makes
the arrival feel launched), which is the profile again. Second
refinement, unprompted: "*is this whole thing kinda the change of bpm
tho? feels like we suddenly doubled*" — they detected harmonic rhythm
as perceived tempo with the BPM never moving (chain: chord per cell;
plateaus: every other cell; second half: per cell again = the felt
doubling). The dip is also the one adjacent-cell strike inside the
plateaus — a single double-speed beat right before the rate doubles
for real, so the wind-up works in pitch and in rate at once. Harmonic
rhythm ≠ tempo is a distinction many trained players never make
explicit; the untrained ear surfaced it as a question about BPM.
