Notes are entered by position, not by letter, so the layout is the same on any keyboard. Entering a note advances the cursor two steps, and so does clearing one: a step is a sixteenth, sixteen of them are a bar, and eighths are what most of this is thought in — so writing straight ahead lays notes on 1, 3, 5, 7 and counts nothing. A sixteenth is one deliberate movement away: step back with an arrow and write there. Nothing is snapped to a grid; it is two steps on from wherever the cursor is, and it wraps around the page as the arrows do.

A note may also last longer than the step it begins on. Every note is written as a sixteenth, as it always was, and is made longer afterwards — with − and +, or by keeping down whatever wrote it — whereupon it goes on sounding across the steps it covers rather than being struck again on each one: one note, one ending. On the page the steps it covers carry no writing of their own but a single stroke down the column, from the note to a small foot where it stops; in the drawing the note is simply one longer bar. A note cannot ring through another note of its own voice — a line is one line — so a note written into the middle of a hold shortens it for as long as it is there, and taking that note away gives the hold its full length back. Nothing is ever rewritten behind you. A note may ring past the last step of the loop, in which case it comes round with the loop and is drawn again at the head of the page: the seam is a place a note can sound across rather than a wall. Where the two voices sound together the interval is named as before, and a note that is merely ringing counts as sounding — so a voice moving under a held one is written where it moves, which is the whole shape of a suspension. A page written before any of this opens exactly as it always did, every note one step long, and a page with nothing held in it is saved exactly as it always was.

The page holds two voices — a lead and a bass — and they play together, from the one clock. They are written one at a time: the strip above the page names both and marks the one in hand, the column gives each a place of its own beside the step number, and in the roll they are drawn in the same field, the voice you are not in a shade back. Tab changes hands. Everything else — the key, the tempo, the loop, the whole page — the two share; only the notes and the timbre are their own. The bass is rounder and darker, so a bass written up in the melody's octave still sounds like the bass. Solo (O) and mute (P) act on the voice you are in; solo, if any voice has it, is what decides, and a change is heard on the next step without a restart. A page written before there were two voices opens exactly as it always did, with a silent bass under it.

The drawing names what it draws, in its margin rather than on top of itself. The home note of the page — the tonic of its key — is ruled across the roll at every octave in view and named once, at the side of its line, with the word home under the name: in C major those rules are the Cs, in G major the Gs, in A minor the As, and they move the moment the key does. Home is therefore a line you can see, and how far a note sits from it is how far it is from home. The octave is numbered the international way, so that C4 is middle C and the page runs from C2 to C6. Place a note, or move one, and a line of its own is drawn at that note's pitch, parallel to those rules and named beside them; it holds for a moment and then fades, so the pitch is said when you ask for it and the drawing is left alone the rest of the time. The column has always named its notes in the same alphabet, a dash standing in where there is no sharp, so that the two columns line up. Where both voices sound on the same step the roll also writes the interval between them, on a hairline tie drawn from the one bar to the other, so that the name belongs to that pair of notes and to nothing else however far apart the voices are. Below the octave the short names are the usual ones — P1, m2, M2, m3, M3, P4, TT, P5, m6, M6, m7, M7, P8 — and wider than an octave the compound name is used: the simple number plus seven for every octave crossed, so a minor third and an octave is m10, a fifth and an octave is P12, two octaves is P15. The tritone keeps its name and takes the compound number with it: TT, then TT11, then TT18. Only the distance is named, never the direction, so it reads the same whichever voice is on top. Nothing here judges: no interval is coloured, ranked, or marked as wanting anything. The page says what the two notes are; what they are worth is for the ear. K puts the whole lot away and brings it back.

The key and the tempo both belong to the page you are on, and so to the workspace it lives in: each quest keeps its own, free play keeps its own, and changing one changes nothing anywhere else. Either may be changed while the loop runs; the change is heard at the next step, without a restart. A quest's page arrives already tuned — its own key and its own tempo, chosen to suit its constraint — so there is nothing to decide before writing; change them here whenever you like, and the change stays with that quest. Free play always begins in C major at 112.

Every quest is a workspace: a whole page of its own — steps, title, tempo, loop and key — and one more page, free play, belongs to no quest. The page in front of you saves itself into the workspace you are in, on every change, as it always did; choosing another quest simply puts a different page in front of you. Nothing is bound, nothing is loaded, nothing is copied, and so nothing can be written over. The margins of this page carry the same thing quietly while you work: on the left every workspace and where you are, on the right the constraint you are under. A narrow window drops both rather than squeeze the column.

Below the eight, under their own tab, sit the drills: short études written into the quest log from outside, each with its own page already on it. They are workspaces like any other — enter one, write, mark it complete — and when the folio is served, a drill added to the log while this page is open simply appears in the list, quietly, without a reload and without disturbing a line of what you have written.

The board is read one lesson at a time. The strip above the list has a tab for every lesson that actually has quests in it and one for the drills, and a lesson that does not exist yet has no tab: the moment its quests are in the log, its tab is simply there. Nothing has to be told which lesson a quest belongs to — the eight standing quests are Lesson 1, a delivered quest may say which lesson it is for, and one that says nothing joins the newest lesson on the board. A quest kept to hand (F) is pinned to the head of its lesson and to the head of the left margin, where it is in view while you write; shift with up or down moves a quest and it stays where it is put. Both marks travel with the quest log, and a log written before either existed opens exactly as it always did.

The background can also be the workspace's own: shift + B once past the sea, and the scene behind the sheet becomes the picture that belongs to the quest you are in. There is one for most of the board and none for free play, and a workspace without one simply stays on paper — nothing waits for a picture and nothing moves when one arrives.

Opened from a file, the folio keeps everything in the browser alone. Started with folio.cmd and opened at http://localhost:4173, it also keeps the whole quest log in a real file — quests/quest‑log.json — written a couple of seconds after every change, and read back when it opens. The footer says · synced while the quest log is open. If the server goes away the work carries on in the browser and the footer says so; the next change tries again.

A copy of the folio can also be put on an ordinary web host and sent to somebody. There is no server behind that copy, so it never sends anything anywhere: it reads the quest log that travelled with it once, on a browser that has nothing of its own yet, and after that everything the reader writes is theirs and stays in their browser. The footer says · read‑only copy while the quest log is open.

The cross hotbar, borrowed: a trigger raises the eight slots, letting go gives the d‑pad back to navigation. Twenty‑four semitones, every pitch of two octaves, counted up from the octave in the header; entry goes on two steps — an eighth, as on the keyboard — and sounds the note. A DualSense and an Xbox pad report the same positions. A browser notices a pad only after you press one of its buttons: press start if nothing answers — the settings crossbar coming up is the pad saying hello. Sound may wait for one keyboard touch.

Bare, the face buttons act alone as in the game — □ clears and ○ returns to the top; raise a trigger and all four are notes again.

Relative entry changes only the bare face buttons, and only what they mean while you write: you give the shape — up, down, again, rest — and the page keeps ordinary notes, as always. A move is one step of the key shown in the header, counted from the last note that sounds before the cursor; rests are stepped over, and the search wraps around the loop. An empty page begins on the tonic. A note that lies outside the key moves first to the nearest note of the key in the direction you asked, and that counts as the step. In contour entry the two triggers are not a crossbar but the two ends of the note under the cursor — L2 its start, R2 its end, moved by the d‑pad's ← and → — because the face buttons are already how one writes here. The crossbar is absolute entry's, whole: hold a trigger there and the eight slots are absolute pitches, as they always were. Set the key on this page.

Settings

Start raised this; start puts it down again, and so does ○. While it is up the eight slots are items instead of notes — the d‑pad on the left, the face buttons on the right, each read left, up, right, down. ↑ and ↓ walk the workspaces in the left margin: the highlight moves and you are already in that workspace, so there is nothing to confirm and no list to open first. ← and → go round the voices. The view — the roll or the column — is not here: it is R3, and F2, where it has always been, and neither is the key: that is F1.
